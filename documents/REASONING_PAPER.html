Reading and causal inference — suggested 16 minutes

P1 — The later closing time

A town tested whether keeping reading rooms open later would increase visits. Eight branches volunteered. A lottery assigned four to close two hours later for six weeks; the other four retained their usual hours. All branches counted entries using the same procedure, and visitors were not told which branches were being evaluated. Total visits rose at the later-closing branches and fell slightly at the others. The later-closing branches also reported that some evening visitors had previously used another branch earlier in the day.

The coordinator called the result encouraging but resisted two claims. First, a rise at the selected branches need not equal a rise across the town: a person changing branches can raise one count while lowering another. Second, the volunteers might differ from branches that declined the experiment. The lottery improves comparison within the participating group; it does not turn volunteers into a representative sample of every branch.

For a second trial, the coordinator proposed recording anonymous repeat-visit patterns across all branches and inviting branches that had not volunteered. She also wanted to examine whether any change survived after the novelty of the longer hours faded. None of those proposed measurements had yet been collected.

P2 — The archive that survived

An historian found a collection of letters written by residents of a coastal village during a difficult winter. Most surviving letters praised the village council for distributing food fairly. A popular account treated this as proof that nearly every resident approved of the council. The historian noticed, however, that the collection had been assembled by a council secretary who asked satisfied households to send accounts of their experiences. The archive contained no list of households approached, no response rate, and almost no letters from people who could not write.

This did not make the letters false. Several described specific deliveries that were independently recorded in a warehouse ledger. But confirming those deliveries did not establish how common the writers' satisfaction was. Evidence can be reliable about an event while being selective about whose interpretation survives.

The historian therefore separated three questions: whether particular deliveries happened, whether the distribution rules were applied consistently, and how residents felt about the rules. The ledger could help with the first question and, with suitable comparisons, the second. It could not directly reveal unrecorded opinions. She recommended seeking complaints, oral accounts preserved elsewhere, and records from households omitted from the collection, while warning that a second selective archive would not automatically solve the problem.

P3 — A warning rule

A valley committee must decide when to issue a flood warning. Its written rule says to issue a warning if and only if both the river gauge is above its threshold and an upstream observer reports rapid rise. The two conditions are evaluated at the same scheduled checkpoint. The rule was chosen to limit unnecessary warnings, not because either signal alone was believed to be useless. Gauge readings are logged automatically; observer reports arrive by radio and can be delayed.

During a practice session, the gauge exceeded its threshold but the observer's message had not arrived. A recorder wrote, "The warning conditions are not yet established." A supervisor replaced this with, "There is no flood risk." The committee later rejected that replacement. A missing report is not the same as a report of no rapid rise, and the warning rule is not a definition of whether a flood can occur.

The committee retained the rule for the exercise but required separate labels for a negative report, a missing report, and a positive report. It also asked for a later study comparing warnings with actual outcomes. Until that study exists, the exercise provides information about compliance with the stated procedure, not a numerical estimate of how accurately it predicts floods.

P4 — Choosing without probabilities

A small island must choose one storage plan before the next season. Planners agree that the season will be dry, ordinary, or wet, but they have no agreed probabilities for these cases. They measure each plan by a loss index, where lower is better. Plan L has losses of 2 in a dry season, 5 in an ordinary season, and 11 in a wet season. Plan M has losses of 6, 6, and 6 respectively. Plan N has losses of 10, 4, and 3 respectively. These values include every consequence the exercise asks the planners to consider.

One committee member wants to minimize the worst possible loss. Another wants to minimize the average loss, while assigning equal weight to the three seasons. Both preferences are coherent decision rules, but equal weights are an additional choice, not evidence that the seasons are equally likely. A third member says a plan should be discarded whenever another plan has no greater loss in any season and a smaller loss in at least one.

The chair asks members to state their rules before seeing which plan each rule favors. She notes that agreement about the table need not produce agreement about the decision. Disagreement can arise from different treatment of uncertainty rather than from a calculation error.

P5 — Copies and independence

A seed archive stored duplicate samples in two rooms of the same building. After a roof failure damaged both rooms, a review found that the duplication had protected against a mislabeled drawer but not against a building-wide event. The director proposed sending one copy to a distant archive. A colleague replied that distance alone would not guarantee independence: both archives might use the same faulty drying procedure or depend on the same supplier of storage containers.

The review distinguished copying an object from diversifying the ways its copies could fail. A second copy is useful when the failures it can survive differ from those threatening the first. The reviewers did not argue that all shared procedures should be abandoned. Standard labels, for example, could make samples easier to locate without being responsible for the roof failure. They asked instead which dependencies could damage both copies and what evidence supported each proposed safeguard.

The final recommendation was to retain clear common labels, separate the physical sites, and test the drying procedure using an independent method. It was a proposal, not a completed intervention. No subsequent survival data were available. The director therefore described the changes as responses to identified vulnerabilities, not as proof that future loss had become impossible.
